Six!

1

The dig: every tap is a step down and a new slope

A run starts with a hexagon resting on top of a tall tower that is exactly five cells wide and packed with interlocking coloured blocks. Tapping a block deletes it instantly. Gravity does the rest: pieces above drop into the hole, the hexagon settles, and the camera follows it down. There is no timer and no enemy — the only way to lose is to let the hexagon roll off the side.

The defining choice is not whether to tap but where. Points are flat — five per block, anywhere — so the decision is purely about geometry: which removal lowers the hexagon without tilting the surface it stands on.

One tap, three consequences

Schematic, not to scale: the real tower is five cells wide and the hexagon spans a little more than two.Dashed outline = the block just tapped.

Because every block is worth the same, Six! never asks the player to hunt for valuable pieces. What it asks is to read support. The hexagon is wider than two cells, so it usually rests on two or three blocks at once. Remove the one under its middle and it sinks; remove one under a single corner and the surface becomes a ramp. On a ramp a hexagon does what hexagons do — it tips from face to face until something stops it or the tower ends.

That makes the cost of a mistake immediate and legible. In one recorded run the player's very first tap removed a block under one side; the hexagon tipped, rolled over the left edge and the run ended on five points. Later runs show the opposite habit: tapping in the column directly under the hexagon, keeping its two lower corners on the same height, and only then widening the hole.

Speed changes the trade-off. Gravity resolves continuously, so fast taps outrun the player's ability to check the slope. The game lets frantic players do it anyway — it accepted rapid multi-finger taps without any cooldown in the recordings — and that tension between careful digging and greedy tapping is the main thing Pocket Gamer's review meant by "somewhere between cerebral and twitchy".

2

First minute: one hand, one word, then the floor gives way

The first launch is almost empty: a score pill reading 0 | 0, a star badge at 0, one green Play button and a row of small round buttons. The Challenge button and the video offer are not there yet. One tap on Play drops the player straight into a tower.

The teaching chain

  1. Instruction: hand + "Tap!" on the top block. In the first recording the hand stayed up for about six seconds before the first block went; a transcript of the player's commentary has him tapping the hexagon first.
  2. Required action: tap the block; it bursts into small square debris of its own colour and a "5" floats up.
  3. Safe practice: the hand moves on to the next block for the first few taps; the top of a fresh tower is flat, so early taps rarely tilt anything.
  4. First independent decision: once the hand is gone, the hexagon is in a notch and the player must choose which neighbour to remove.
  5. First failure: the first recorded run ended at 25 points, about six seconds after the first tap, when the hexagon rolled off the right side.

What the first run does not explain

Nothing on screen says what the dashed numbered lines are for, why the screen turns black after a fall, or that tapping still pays during that black screen. Players discover star lines when the badge bursts into confetti, and they discover Last Call by accident — the creator of the iPhone recording only started mass-tapping in it about ten runs in. A notification-permission prompt from iOS interrupts the menu after one high-score run; the platform, not the game, owns that dialog.

Loops: a 1-second decision inside a 15-second run inside a star collection

The game stacks four loops, each measured in a different unit: a tap (well under a second), a run (seconds), a session of runs separated by one-tap restarts, and a long arc of stars spent on skins and challenge towers.

Two things are missing by design. There is no in-run power: skins change only the hexagon's look, and stars buy nothing that affects physics. And there is no difficulty selector or upgrade path; the tower itself is the whole difficulty. Whether the endless tower becomes harder with depth is not visible in the recordings — the composition at 150+ points looks like the composition at the top, only in cooler colours.

4

Points pay for removal; stars pay for depth

Six! runs two counters that reward different things. The score counts blocks destroyed. The star bank counts how far down the hexagon has travelled. A careful digger and a frantic tapper can end a run with the same stars and very different scores.

Score: a flat +5

Every floating number seen in the endless recordings is a "5", and every finished run ended on a multiple of five (5, 25, 35, 45, 55, 85, 90, 100, 125, 150, 170, 180). The counter rolls up rather than jumping, which is why screenshots catch odd values such as 14 or 54. The HUD always shows best | current, and the moment current passes best a large "Highscore!" flashes across the tower.

Stars: one per line passed

Numbered dashed lines sit on the left margin of the tower. A line still worth a star shows a small orange star under its number. When the hexagon descends past it, the star flies to the badge, the badge bursts into confetti and the line keeps only its number. In one measured frame consecutive lines were about ten cell-rows apart. Stars persist across runs and are the only currency.

Two details make stars generous. They count during the fall: a hexagon rolling off just above a line still collects it on the way down. And they are banked even when a challenge level fails.

5

Last Call: the failure that keeps paying

When the hexagon leaves the tower the screen fades grey for a fraction of a second, then every colour inverts: the white world turns black, warm blocks turn cyan and green, and "Last Call!" appears under the score. For about three seconds the tower is still live. Every block tapped still bursts and still pays five points. Then the menu returns with the final score.

Last Call converts the end of the run into its loudest moment. A careful player gains a block or two; a player who has learned the rule mashes the whole screen.

The iPhone recording shows that shift. Late in the session the player switched to multi-finger tapping during runs and Last Call alike. In one run the counter never stopped: it read 110 as the screen greyed, passed 170 before the inversion ended and fired "Highscore!" on the way; that run set his best at 180. In the next, the counter showed 95 in the last dark frame and the menu settled on 125. Because the counter animates behind the true score, the exact split between pre-fall and Last Call points cannot be measured from footage; the direction is unambiguous.

For design, Last Call does three things at once. It removes the sting of failure by giving the player an action at the moment of loss. It makes the high score depend partly on raw tapping speed, which keeps the leaderboard from being a pure patience test. And it teaches the colour language of the game: inversion means "the hexagon is gone, but the blocks are still yours".

6

Challenge towers have a floor

Challenge mode turns the endless dig into finite puzzles. Each level is a short tower standing on a grey pedestal, with three numbered lines on the way down. The score disappears from the HUD; three outline stars take its place.

The pedestal changes the decision. In endless mode the bottom never arrives, so a lopsided hole is only dangerous until the next flat row. On a pedestal tower the last blocks are the hardest: the player must remove every piece under the hexagon while keeping it centred on a slab barely wider than it is. Every completion seen ended with three stars, consistent with the third line sitting at the pedestal's top; a failed attempt keeps whatever lines it passed.

The Daily track repeats the same format with three nodes that unlock in order and a countdown to the next set (23:58, 23:47 and 14:58 were all seen). Completing all three in a day is one of the nine achievements. Levels in the walkthrough take roughly 20 seconds each, so a Daily set is a two-minute errand.

Cleared nodes show gold stars; the next node is unlocked and the rest stay padlocked. The 99-level count and the linear lock are clear; the content of the later towers is not covered by the recordings.

7

Stars buy looks; ads pay the bills

Stars have exactly one sink: hexagon skins. The commercial layer sits beside the game rather than inside it — banners, interstitials, a star video and a one-off purchase to remove ads.

Skins, before → refusal

With stars arriving at one or two per ordinary run, a 30 ★ skin is ten to twenty runs away — or one video: the menu's red button offered exactly +30 ★ on iOS (it was never pressed in the footage). That pricing makes the video the obvious shortcut to the first skin. A bought skin (an orange hexagon with a maroon rim) appears in the walkthrough; its purchase flow was not recorded.

Achievements

Nine achievements, three families: score (500/1000/3000/5000 points), stars in a single game (3/6/9/12) and one Daily goal. The star family rewards deep single runs, the opposite of Last Call mashing, so the two goals pull players toward both styles.

The commercial layer

Launch coverage described the offer as free-to-play with ads and a one-time $4 purchase to remove them. When Zynga bought Gram Games in 2018, Sensor Tower's analysis of the deal noted that advertising revenue from titles such as 1010! and Six! was outside its bookings estimate — a reminder that the business was ad-shaped, which explains the generous, frequent restarts and the video that pays exactly one skin.

8

Feel: flat colour, instant pops, a world that inverts

Look

White background, flat blocks with a lighter inner rim, no texture, no shadows. Colour encodes depth: the top of the tower is yellow and orange, then red, pink, magenta, purple and violet as the hexagon descends; the part of the tower above the camera fades to white. The default hexagon is cyan with a pale centre — the only cool colour in a warm stack, so it never gets lost. Thin, light numerals for the score and a black pill for best | current give the HUD its calm.

Motion

Removal has no anticipation: the block is gone on the tap and replaced by a puff of small squares of its colour, plus a "5" that rises and fades. The rest is honest physics — pieces slump, tumble off the sides, and the hexagon tips from face to face on a slope. The camera eases downward to keep the hexagon in the upper half of the screen. Star claims fly a star into the badge and burst confetti there; a fall triggers the grey fade and the inversion.

Sound

The recordings carry presenter voice-over over most of the play, and the game audio was not separately heard for this teardown. A listener's notes describe a short pop on each block, a chime on star lines, a low tone when the hexagon drops and no music; those descriptions remain unconfirmed. The menu's speaker button is a single on/off switch.

Readability

  • Owner: the hexagon is the only cool, outlined shape in a warm field.
  • Interactivity: every block is tappable; there is no inert scenery to mistake for a target.
  • Danger: a slope is visible as geometry; the game adds no warning before a roll.
  • Reward ahead: numbered lines with a star, on the left margin away from the thumb.
  • State change: the inversion is impossible to miss even with the sound off.

Product, audience and reception

Six! was a launch-week chart hit from a studio known for calm block puzzles, built for anyone with a thumb and a spare twenty seconds.

Offer and audience

Gram Games released Six! on iOS on Friday 26 August 2016 with an App Store feature on day one; an Android version followed within weeks (the Android recording here dates from 14 September 2016). It was free with ads, with a $4 one-time Remove Ads purchase. The audience is broad and casual: no reading, no account, no setup, one input. What the design demands is spatial judgement under mild time pressure plus, for high scores, tapping speed — a tension the studio's earlier hits (1010!, Merged!) did not have.

Current availability

On 8 October 2026 the App Store lookup for the game's iOS ID returned no listing in the US, UK, Turkish, Australian and German storefronts, and the Google Play page for com.gramgames.six returned 404. A third-party tracker last records an iOS update on 27 November 2021. The game appears to be delisted; no official notice was found.

Reception snapshot

  • Steam players: not applicable — Six! was never a Steam game.
  • Store ratings: no current App Store or Google Play rating could be read because the listings are gone (checked 8 Oct 2026). A third-party tracker shows 4.5 from only 3 reviews in one small storefront — too small and undated to describe reception.
  • Critics: no Metacritic or OpenCritic aggregate found. Pocket Gamer (Matt Suckley, 1 Sep 2016, iOS) gave 4.5/5, praising the simple tap mechanic that needs both quick fingers and planning and the lean package of endless and challenge modes; its only caveat was that the game is twitchier than Gram's calmer puzzlers.
  • Launch momentum: PocketGamer.biz (1 Sep 2016) reported 2 million downloads in the first week and the No. 1 spot in the US App Store free chart; GamesBeat (3 Sep 2016) reported the same milestone. These are publisher-supplied figures.
  • Interpretation: one strongly positive specialist review and a publisher-reported launch spike are all that can be verified. They show the idea landed instantly with a broad audience; they do not establish long-term retention or revenue, and the later delisting suggests the game did not remain a priority after the 2018 acquisition.

Takeaways

Flat rewards make geometry the decision

Because every block pays +5, the player never weighs value — only support. Variable rewards would have split attention away from the slope.

Give the loss an action

Last Call lets a failed run end with the player's busiest three seconds. The failure is visible (inversion), short, and still pays.

Two counters, two playstyles

Score rewards removal speed; stars reward depth. Achievements push on both, so careful and frantic players each have a ladder.

Finite levels need a floor

The challenge pedestal turns an endless dig into a puzzle with a precise last move, without adding a single new rule.

Core statements

  • C1 — Support decides survival. The player chooses one block to delete; it pays +5 wherever it is; the stack and hexagon re-settle, and the resulting surface (level or sloped) decides whether the hexagon stays for the next choice.
  • C2 — Depth pays stars. Passing a numbered line pays +1 persistent star, so lowering the hexagon (not merely tapping) advances the long arc; a lopsided dig is the price of speed.
  • C3 — The loss is still live. When the hexagon leaves the tower, a ≈3 s colour-inverted Last Call keeps every block tappable for +5, so the end of a run is its busiest moment and can set the best score.
  • C4 — Finite towers have a floor. In Challenge, clearing the tower so the hexagon rests on the pedestal completes the level; stars equal lines passed and are banked even on failure; stars buy only cosmetic skins.

Characteristic episode E1 (S2 0:58–1:16; fig-line-before → fig-pk-star → fig-pk-rolloff → fig-lastcall-start → fig-lastcall-tap → fig-pk-menu-after): fresh endless tower, star bank 3 → the player digs under the hexagon, passes line 1 at ~54–60 points (bank 4) → digs an uneven hole; at 80 the hexagon slides off the left edge above line 2 → grey fade, inversion, "Last Call!" → one more block tapped → menu 100 | 85, bank 4.

Art direction and signals

Style thesis: keep the source's flat, white, colour-coded clarity and make it crisper: vector blocks with a 2–3 px lighter inner rim at native device resolution, soft but small debris, generous HUD numerals. Keep: warm depth gradient vs a cool hexagon; numbered left-margin lines with stars; the full-screen inversion for Last Call. Change: no ad strip (the reclaimed top area holds the HUD), a clearer pause icon, larger touch targets in menus, a calm result card. Camera and framing (portrait): HUD in the top 12%; hexagon at ~40% height; the tower's lower half is where the thumb works; numbers and particles never cover the hexagon's supporting blocks; menu buttons in the bottom third. Anticipation / impact / recovery: deletion is instant (no wind-up), impact is the debris puff and settling, recovery is the stack coming to rest; line claims get a short star flight; the fall gets the grey fade.
