01 / The decision

The tower is a record of error.

The score rises while the landing area usually shrinks. Progress and survivability move in opposite directions.

The player does not steer the slab toward a target. They watch a crossing and choose when to freeze it. The surviving intersection becomes both the reward for this input and the target for the next one.

Alternating axes makes the cost accumulate in two dimensions. Losing width on one placement leaves depth unchanged; the next placement tests the other edge. Eventually a tower can become a narrow strip or a tiny column. The cut is not just an animation: it changes future legal overlap.

Waiting is an available tactic. The moving block can cross, reverse at an endpoint and approach again. A player can abandon a bad pass without spending a resource. The game pressures judgment through motion and shrinking space, rather than an evidenced countdown.

Conceptual geometry, not measured source dimensions. For equal slabs moving on one axis, overlap = max(0, current dimension − absolute offset), before perfect snapping. At the same speed, a smaller dimension offers less time with any overlap. Depth multiplies the effect on area.

The most useful strategy is to preserve options. A lucky late tap may keep the run alive, yet leave a target so small that the next crossing is harder. A clean placement saves geometry as well as adding height.

02 / Consequence

The lost piece explains the mistake.

The geometry shows the size and direction of the error without a separate health bar.

These ordered states isolate an actual cut, rather than two unrelated placements. The excess goes down; the overlap stays. The second state already includes the next slab approaching on the perpendicular track. The player receives three pieces of information in one response: the placement counted, this much area was lost, and the next decision uses the smaller top.

There is no need to require a general-purpose mesh-boolean or physics engine. An axis-aligned cuboid can be partitioned into a retained prism and a falling prism. What matters is a truthful boundary, coherent volume, gravity-like descent and a readable surviving surface.

03 / Recovery

Precision can reverse the loss.

A perfect sequence offers something more valuable than points: room for the next input.

A clean placement produces a white contact outline. Longer accurate play adds layered outward squares and can expand the footprint. The recovery opportunity makes a damaged run worth continuing: a narrow tower is not merely a countdown to an unavoidable loss.

The resulting tension is recursive. Pursuing a streak encourages rhythm, but rhythm cannot replace reading the current crossing. Growth changes the surface being aimed at. A successful recovery asks the player to recalibrate rather than mechanically repeat an interval.

The exact snap tolerance, streak threshold, growth increment and cap are not established for these recording builds. The 2016 AndroidGuys review describes growth after eight matching placements; the target must implement recovery, while its explicit tuning remains a design choice.

The reward repairs the resource that makes the core action possible.

A good adaptation preserves this relationship. A streak that only adds a score multiplier would retain celebration and lose the recovery decision. Conversely, unlimited expansion would erase the cumulative cost of errors. Recovery should be attainable from a damaged state without turning imperfect placement into the best strategy.

04 / Failure & restart

Lose the attempt. Keep the lesson.

The terminal tower tells a story. The next attempt restores a broad starting surface, while the record provides a longer horizon.

The inspected sequence continues from a miss into a stable result, then into another controllable attempt. It establishes reset geometry and a retained benchmark; the timing of the player’s touch is not visible, so it does not establish a universal restart latency.

The classic record is deliberately modest: the personal best survives an inferior run. One checked screen reads 8 / BEST 45; another reads 96 / BEST 137. Neither a leaderboard icon nor a high record demonstrates a working online ranking service.

05 / Teaching & signals

The seam matters more than the spectacle.

The source asks the player to learn by looking. The target should make the first useful touch unmistakable.

The title's play triangle has a clear affordance. During play, the slab supplies a continuous demonstration of possible alignment. A contact seam confirms accuracy; a falling strip explains an imperfect result; a complete falling slab explains failure.

No readable written tutorial is present in the selected opening stills. That is a limited observation, not proof that every version lacks one. The target can use a short, dismissing instruction on the moving scene: “Tap when the edges line up.” The first meaningful touch commits a slab rather than navigating a long title flow.

Keep the active slab and support above the thumb's normal landing area. Score, pause and small feedback text should occupy the upper rail. Contrast must survive every palette: a dark cosmetic is allowed to look dark, not hide the collision edge.

Before commitment

Separate active slab from stationary support through position, face shading and an optional fine edge. Direction must remain understandable with sound muted.

After commitment

Keep the retained surface unobscured. Short squares can celebrate accuracy; debris must explain the cut rather than camouflage it.

Ready for the next input

A new slab and the camera follow communicate continuation. Avoid carrying a held touch or a tap on Settings into an unintended second placement.

Core statements and design principles

  1. C1. The player sees a moving slab and support, chooses the commit time, and receives the surviving overlap. That new footprint changes the next placement opportunity.
  2. C2. Imprecision reduces room on the active axis; sustained precision can restore room. Recovery rewards the same skill that caused the initial survival.
  3. C3. A complete miss ends this attempt; retry restores the initial opportunity while a retained record gives later attempts meaning.

Principles (interpretation). Let changing geometry create difficulty before adding systems. Teach by visible contact, keep the error in the world, and reward accuracy with future possibility. Alternate axes to prevent mastery becoming a one-dimensional repeating tap. Keep pacing adjustable through waiting; cosmetic progress must never substitute for learning. These principles describe the classic loop, not a proven retention model.
