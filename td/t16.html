01 / THE FIRST DEALA toy is an offer, not a commitment

The table makes ownership spatial. Put something near yourself, watch the far side respond, then choose whether to accept, demand another object or walk away.

Large cross, plus and check controls make the verbs easy to discover. Their mirrored arrangement makes the opponent appear to use the same rules. The initial instruction is short—drag to trade—so the first offer teaches the interaction through the response it creates. The stronger learning problem comes one beat later: a colourful pile says little about when another demand is safe.

The first refusal is useful teaching only if the player can see what was lost and what survived. A clear rejected result creates a new attempt; a sudden wipe can also look like the game stole the collection. The mobile adaptation needs an explicit distinction between a temporary table offer and owned stock.

The evidence contains two presentation and economy contexts: a cash-and-room version with a vertical meter, and a coin-and-level version with a circular meter. Their exact build numbers are unavailable. The observed states are kept separate; the target combines their relevant systems as a designed adaptation.

02 / BARGAININGDeal quality and willingness are different questions

The meter makes a judgement visible, but it does not explain the opponent’s limit.

Objects carry internal trading meaning rather than a trustworthy retail price. The drawer’s purchase price is another variable again. A laptop box can meet a visually identical box across the table; the theme invites a collection fantasy more than a financial simulation.

One observed deal reaches a positive result after a yellow meter state. That is enough to reject a universal rule that “green is the only legal acceptance state.” The exact mapping from each side’s toys to the needle is not established.

For a mobile game, keep the two decisions readable: is this offer attractive to me? and will the other trader tolerate another request? A single traffic-light meter should not silently answer both.

The defining relationship

Current offer

Objects on each side → perceived deal quality → accept now.

Next request

Potential added value ↔ opponent’s willingness → improve the deal or lose the table.

Analytical model: the trade-off is supported by offer/withdrawal states. No numerical risk curve or expected-value formula is claimed.

The late crowded deal shows the actual cost of pushing too far: the pile disappears, the level remains, and another offer can begin.

The repeated request creates suspense because the player already sees a potential prize. Refusal removes that unfinished opportunity. It need not remove the player’s whole collection to matter. A fast restart preserves the urge to try a more disciplined bargain; without a comprehensible cause, it becomes random button pressing.

Where the illusion becomes fragile

Same-kind gadget offers and dramatic mixed piles reinforce the playful toy-market fantasy. They also expose the danger of a hidden value model: a player may interpret a green judgement as either a fair exchange, a profitable exchange or the opponent’s approval.

The useful improvement is information discipline. Keep the offer’s meaning separate from consent, show incoming goods moving into the player’s possession, and explain an opponent refusal through a reaction tied to the request. Do not claim that an unverified source exploit is the game’s intended strategy.

03 / COLLECTION & RELEASEThe deal feeds two different kinds of progress

More toys change what can be offered. Cash also changes the room around the trader. Those are different rewards: strategic variety and visible ownership.

Quantity badges, star tiers, purchase prices and video offers occupy the same small drawer. Each symbol asks a different question: what do I own, what kind of item is it, and what must I spend to get more? Conflating those numbers makes an already opaque bargain harder to understand.

The reward screen is another negotiation

The cash version distinguishes Earned +50 from a video-marked Claim 150 and an X1–X3 track. A player is asked to make another choice after the deal has finished. The coin version similarly shows a Multiply option and Continue.

This is source monetization, not a requirement for the web game. All base toys, modes and renovation must be reachable in ordinary play. A retained timing-track bonus can be a free optional skill beat; it must never replace the accessible base reward.

The best settlement also makes ownership obvious. “I won” should mean visible incoming objects and a coherent ledger, rather than both piles vanishing under a hand.

Pop-it rounds change the pressure, not the destination

Bargaining asks whether to stop. Popping asks where the next unpopped bubble is. The heart round removes the social bluff and gives each touch a local, legible consequence. Its completion still returns a progression reward, so the sensory break belongs to the same session loop.

The schedule is not proven to alternate strictly or use only odd levels. The full adaptation should choose its pacing deliberately: short sensory rounds between negotiations, plus a safe anytime toy sandbox, instead of mechanically copying an unverified parity rule.

Discovery gives the next deal a reason to exist

A new toy family changes the visual vocabulary of the offer and invites a valuation experiment. Collection progression is strongest when an unlock creates a new decision, not merely another icon or a larger automatic payout.

The target needs controlled scarcity and varied opponents without exhausting the player’s ability to offer anything. A refusal should invite a better bargain; a depleted drawer should still have a clear route back to playable stock.

Renovation turns surplus into a visible place

The checked wall purchase consumes 400 from 450, leaves 50, and exposes a Level 2 offer at 480. The floor’s visible starting cash offer is 300; ad and cash alternatives vary between these two states. This is a connected money sink, not a certified price-scaling law.

No checked frame establishes the fully renovated endpoint or a gameplay bonus from the room. The adaptation can therefore make the room a cosmetic completion goal, with unmistakable before/after changes and a complete affordable upgrade path. It must leave the trading decision intact.

The collection needs meaningful variety; the room needs visible change. Neither should make asking for more the only sensible move.

Core statements

  1. C1. Placing an owned toy creates an offer and a distinguishable far-side response; selection alone does not commit the exchange.
  2. C2. Asking for more can improve the current deal or cause withdrawal; accepting current value remains a meaningful alternative. Quality and willingness are independently readable.
  3. C3. Accepting changes owned inventory and grants a coherent reward once; refusal clears the temporary bargain and permits a usable retry.
  4. C4. Acquired toy variety and coin spending affect later offers and visible collection/room progress; tactile rounds and sandbox feed or relieve that loop without replacing bargaining.

Principles for generating situations

  • Teach the entire offer→response→decision→result loop once, then give the next round a different offer and autonomy.
  • Escalate through different toy roles, preferences, counter-demands and signposted willingness; larger automatic piles alone are not difficulty.
  • Make refusal a legible loss of an unfinished deal and a quick chance to try a better strategy. Preserve the collection fantasy without endless free duplication.
  • Let coin sinks ask whether to buy negotiating variety or improve the room; the room’s benefit is visible expression, not hidden trade power.
  • Reward a completed action with readable ownership and tactile consequence. Sensory rounds change cadence while collection and bargaining remain connected.

Short-session loop / Designed

Entry into a ready starter table → first inspect/place → offer/response/ask/adjust → accept or withdrawal → clear result → reward/unlock or retry → optional spending/pop round → next table. Aim for roughly 20–60 seconds per negotiation and 10–25 seconds for a pop round, varied by the player; these are tuning targets, not measured original durations. Persist coins, stock, collection and room state at coherent checkpoints. Difficulty grows through opponent profiles and distinct toy opportunities, not compulsory daily tasks or a forced timer. The post-completion loop remains open.
