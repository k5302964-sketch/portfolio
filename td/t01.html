Hypercasual Teardown · mobile web adaptation brief

RISEUP

You never touch the balloon. You move the sky out of its way — and every shove that clears the lane also decides where the debris falls next.

A white balloon climbs on its own. One finger, dragged anywhere on the screen, moves a small ring that shoves, lifts and parks falling shapes. The first piece that touches the balloon pops it. The whole game is a negotiation with physics about how much force, and where.

Room 1 · Control

The balloon is not yours — the ring is

Rise Up removes the obvious verb. The balloon climbs straight up the centre of the screen at a constant pace and cannot be steered; the player's only tool is a guard ring that follows the finger's motion from wherever the finger happens to be.

The game's own instruction card puts the whole contract on four panels: protect the balloon with the round guard; touch and drag to move it; you do not have to put your finger on the guard; it can be controlled anywhere on the screen (fig-tutorial). Footage from the Android build, recorded with the system's touch indicator switched on, shows exactly that: the finger dot sits low or off to the side while the ring works higher up (fig-touch-offset, fig-funnel). The ring moves by the finger's displacement, so the thumb never covers the ring or the danger above it.

The ring itself is indestructible and costs nothing to use — no stamina, no cooldown, no second button. It is a kinematic body: it imparts momentum to anything loose and is never pushed back. That makes the design brutally simple to describe and unexpectedly deep to play, because the only thing the player chooses is where the ring is and how fast it arrives.

There is one teaching weakness, and it matters for an adaptation. In the recorded builds the instruction card lives behind the i button on the menu; a run starts with no on-screen prompt, and the ring and the balloon are both small white circles in the same column. Nothing in the first second says which of them answers to the finger.

Three statements carry the game: the ring moves loose pieces, never the balloon; one touch on the balloon ends the run; and altitude is the only score, so the only way to do better is to survive the next room.

A run is a stack of twelve-second rooms

Where the same room came back, it came back in the same colour — the orange ramps twice, the purple hopper twice. Colour is how a returning player recognises the room before the pieces arrive.

Room 3 · The decision

Force is the real choice — a shove creates the next problem

Every room is a small physics puzzle stated in two colours. White and charcoal pieces are loose, fall when disturbed and kill on contact; pure black architecture — funnels, ramps, chute walls, anchors — never moves. The player's question is not “can I hit it?” but “where will it go after I do?”

A fast flick scatters. Small pieces fly, rebound off walls and each other, and can drop back into the column seconds later; a cluster of beads hit hard becomes a rain of beads. A slow, sustained push carries. Heavy discs and boulders can be leaned on and walked off to the side; a lid can be held up. The store copy makes the same point in its own words: you can push everything out of the way, but sending blocks flying makes the balloon harder to protect.

Because the balloon is locked to the centre line and rises at a fixed pace, the player always knows where the danger must not be and roughly when. A piece hanging still at the top edge reaches the balloon's height in roughly two and a half seconds of climb, and sooner if it is already falling; pieces moving toward the lane must be intercepted early, at the top of the screen, so their rebound has time to clear. Good play looks like a goalkeeper working ahead of the balloon, not a bodyguard hugging it.

That also explains why the threat feels personal. The ring is never in danger and never tired, so every pop is the player's own geometry — usually a piece the player moved earlier. The game holds the frame still for about a second after the contact so the culprit is visible; it does not highlight it.

Room 4 · Characteristic episode EP-1

The Hopper: tip the lid and it rains, carry it and it spills over the top

One purple room appeared twice in the same iOS session and was decided both times by the same thing — whether the player hit the lid or carried it. It is the clearest single picture of what Rise Up asks for.

A black chute descends over the balloon's lane. Inside it, a tall column of white beads rests on a white lid. The balloon must rise through the chute, so the beads must leave it somehow. The ring can get under the lid from below.

What the episode proves: the same room is lethal or harmless depending only on the speed and duration of the ring's contact. A rebuild that gives the ring only “hit” behaviour — a high-impulse bumper with no sustained push — cannot reproduce this room.

Room 5 · Failure and retry

One touch ends it — then the game holds still long enough to look

There is no health, no shield and no grace period. The first loose piece to touch the balloon pops it, the screen flashes white and the world freezes; one tap later a new run is climbing.

Android shows the same pattern with a white flash; pieces there may drift slightly during the hold.

The freeze matters more than the burst. For roughly a second the whole arrangement stays on screen, so the cause of failure is visible — usually a piece the player moved earlier. The game does not point at it. In crowded rooms (a bead star, a funnel shower) the culprit is one bead among fifty, and the lesson is easy to miss.

Some deaths then show a continue offer: a dark overlay with a ring timer counting down from 10, “CONTINUE?”, a button with a video icon and, about three-quarters of a second later, “NO, THANKS”. The video icon marks it as a revive paid by watching an ad; no recorded player accepted one. It does not appear after every death: it was shown after the first iOS death at 55 and after Android deaths at 364 and 370, but not after an iOS death at 226 or Android deaths at 106. The trigger is not visible in the footage.

Declining leads straight to the result screen — and in the Android build often to a full-screen interstitial ad for another app before the menu returns. The result screen is the menu: attempt number, best score under a trophy, a level field, “GAME OVER”, the score and the big play diamond, so the next run is one tap away.

Room 6 · Meta and monetisation

Around the climb: challenges, balloons, and the shop

The Android build wraps the endless climb in a small amount of structure — a level mode, a balloon collection, a paid ad-removal — all built from the same room vocabulary.

Challenges

CHALLENGES on the menu opens fixed levels. A level begins on a calm screen with a home button and a tapping-hand hint (fig-challenge-ready). The score is replaced by a progress line with the level number in a diamond (fig-challenge-bar). Level 1 was three rooms — charcoal towers, a green plank rain, a cyan field of dotted rows — about 43 seconds in all, ending on a teal “COMPLETED” card with three stars and a greyed balloon (fig-challenge-complete). Failing level 2 showed the same continue offer; declining restarted level 2 from its beginning at once. What the stars measure was not shown.

Balloons

A carousel of balloons, each with an unlock condition and a progress counter, all earned by play: score 500 (completed), 1000, 2000 and 4000 points (555 of each), a play-count goal, “Play with 5 balloons (1/5)” and “Play with all bubbles! (1/10)” — which implies ten balloons. Two defects are visible: the play-count card reads “Play 200 Times!” over a counter of 20/300, and pressing SELECT on the completed striped balloon changes nothing — it never becomes SELECTED, though the player tries repeatedly across the session.

Money

The listing says “Contains ads · In-app purchases”. The menu's NO ADS tab opens a Google Play sheet for “No Ads (Rise Up)” at $3.19 on the recording account's Australian store. Ads appear as interstitials after some game overs and behind the continue offer. A “HAVING FUN? ★★★ RATE NOW” card also interrupts a result screen.

Room 7 · Product, audience, reception

A one-verb hit that grew into a long-running ad-supported product

Rise Up launched in March 2018 as a free solo-developer game and charted within weeks; eight years later it is an Azur Games title with 100M+ Android installs — and its player reviews are dominated by complaints about ads, not about the game.

The offer, then and now

In the July 2018 Android recording the listing shows Serkan Özyılmaz as developer, “Contains ads · In-app purchases”, #2 in Top Games Arcade, 10 million downloads and 4.1 stars from 117,835 ratings (fig-store-2018). On 13 April 2018 it was among the top free iPhone apps in the United States and the Netherlands (AFP Relaxnews via Inquirer, App Annie data).

On 8 October 2026 the Google Play listing names Casual Azur Games, 100M+ downloads, “Contains ads · In-app purchases”, and promises many levels with level 100 as the goal. The App Store listing (“Rise Up! Protect the Balloon”, seller AI Games FZ, © Azur Games) sells No Ads at $2.99, shield and “super guard” packs at $1.99–$4.99, and a weekly premium membership. Boosters arrived in 2025 according to its version notes.

Audience

Declared: anyone who wants a reflex test — the copy leans on difficulty, claiming that only 1% of players reach level 100. Demanded: one finger, full attention for 10–40 second runs, high tolerance for sudden failure and the patience to learn rooms by dying in them. Fit: strong for short-session players; the cost is that the game teaches almost nothing explicitly and relies on repetition.

Room 8 · Development & production

The core never changed; the frame around it kept growing

Between the two 2018 builds and today, the verb, the balloon and the single-touch failure stayed fixed. What changed is the number of rooms, the modes and the business layer.

The production principle visible here is unusually clean: a single, legible verb survived eight years of commercial reshaping because every addition — modes, skins, levels, boosters — was built as more rooms or more wrapping around the same ring. The defects visible in the 2018 footage (the dead SELECT button, the off-by-one record, a stray 0/55 flashing during one restart fade, a play-count card that disagrees with its counter) are exactly the kind of small trust breaks an adaptation should not inherit.

Build prompt — Rise Up adaptation (working title “Updraft”)

Build the complete mobile web game specified in this teardown's Build layer: a portrait, one-finger physics game in which a white balloon rises on its own up the centre of the screen and the player drags a guard ring — from anywhere on the screen, by relative motion — to shove, lift and park loose white pieces out of its lane. Keep the defining choice (where the ring meets a piece, and whether it flicks or carries) and its consequence: the first loose piece that touches the balloon pops it and ends the run; score is altitude only. Deliver the whole game in this build: Endless Climb (teaching room, then 40 numbered rooms drawn per run from a library of at least 24 rooms in difficulty tiers, a summit at room 40 and an endless Beyond), 45 handcrafted Challenges of three rooms each with stars, a play-earned collection of 10 balloons, result/retry flow with best score, Settings with English/Russian and separate Music and SFX sliders, and the bug.games plan in #bug-games. The characteristic episode is the Hopper (EP-1; fig-hopper-hold … fig-hopper-over): it must be lost by tipping the lid and won by carrying it. Design the first 20 minutes with #roblox-hooks: first drag within 5 seconds, a named room and its colour at every cloud band, a visible BEST line, and a held frame after each pop that outlines the culprit. It must play in a portrait browser window or feed iframe from the first touch, read without sound, and ship as one self-contained offline HTML. B3 · Core statements (must hold in the adaptation)
  1. C1 The player decides where the ring meets a loose piece before it reaches the balloon's lane; the contact transfers momentum, so the piece leaves the lane — or rebounds into it later. The positions left behind decide the next seconds.
  2. C2 Force is a choice: a fast flick scatters pieces; slow sustained pressure carries them. The Hopper (EP-1) is lost by the first and won by the second.
  3. C3 The first loose piece that touches the balloon ends the run; the score is altitude, so surviving the current room is the only way to improve — and the next run re-rolls the rooms, so learning transfers through recognition.

B8 · Art direction, signals and assets

Style thesis. Keep the source's flat, geometric sky — pastel room colours, white loose pieces, black fixed architecture, a white balloon with knot and string, cloud bands, a faint room numeral — and refine it rather than reskin it. The look is the rule system: two colours tell the player what moves and what kills.

What changes and why. The ring gets its own identity so it cannot be read as a hazard or as the balloon: thicker white stroke, a tinted inner fill in one constant accent colour, a soft shadow and a brief squash on contact. Pieces get slightly softened edges and a 1–2 px darker outline against light skies for readability on small screens. Room names appear on the cloud band.

Camera and framing. Portrait column; the balloon at ≈72% height; the area above it is the reading zone and must never be covered. HUD in the top 8%: score left, BEST under it, room counter right, pause icon right. The thumb lives in the lower third; nothing important is drawn there except the first-run hint. Pop and result cards centre over the frozen scene.

B10 · Settings: language and audio (Designed requirement)

A small Settings entry (gear on the menu and in pause) with an English/Russian switch applied to the whole interface — hints, room names, result card, Challenges, balloons, errors and any offer states — and two independent touch-sized sliders: Music and Sound effects. Language and both levels are saved as device preferences. A slider at 0 is truly silent; changing one never changes the other. Settings are never a first-run obstacle.
